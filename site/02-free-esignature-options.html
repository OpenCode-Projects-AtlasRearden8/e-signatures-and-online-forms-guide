Chapter 2: Free Legal E-Signature Options

You almost never need to pay to sign something. Between what is already installed on your devices and a handful of established free services, the paid tiers of e-signature tools matter mainly to businesses routing dozens of documents.

This chapter is a decision guide: pick the situation, get the free answer.

First rule: the tool someone sent you is usually the tool to use

When a landlord, hospital, or employer sends a signature request, they send it through a service (DocuSign, Dropbox Sign, Adobe Acrobat Sign, SignWell, Fargo, and so on). The email or text contains a “Sign here” button. Using that button is correct and free for you — the sender pays for the service. You open the link, review, click, and the completed copy comes back to your inbox automatically.

So: most of the time your only job is to recognize the request as legitimate (Chapter 7) and click through.

The rest of this chapter is for the other case: you have a PDF and need to sign or fill it yourself.

Option A: What is already on your device (always free, no account)

Device Tool How to get there Best for
Mac Preview (built-in) Open PDF → Markup toolbar → Sign → trackpad/finger or camera The single best free option for Apple users
iPhone / iPad Markup (built-in) Open PDF → Markup pencil icon → “+” → Signature Signing on the go
Windows Adobe Acrobat Reader (free) Get it from adobe.com/acrobat/pdf-reader Signature plus certificate-based tamper evidence
Windows Microsoft Edge (built-in) Open PDF → Draw/add text with “Add text” Fast typing-and-tapping of fields
Android Adobe Fill & Sign or Acrobat — free Play Store, from Adobe Phone signing with saved signature
Android Google Drive’s PDF tools — free Open PDF in Drive → annotate Light edits and typing
Linux Adobe Acrobat Reader (where available), Okular, Xournal++ Your distro’s package manager Desktop and hand-drawn signing

Why built-ins first: no account, no uploads, no email address to give away. The document never leaves your device. That is both a privacy win and a scam-proofing win — there is no request email to imitate.

Option B: Free e-signature services (when you want a service’s extras)

These are real, established companies with genuinely free tiers. When you use them, you get an audit trail, a log of who signed when, and completed copies returned to your inbox.

SignWell — free plan, a handful of documents per month, unlimited templates not included, but fine for occasional signer needs. Clean interface, no credit card for the free tier.

DocuSign free tier — the industry standard offers a free way to sign and occasionally send. Good when the other party expects “DocuSign” branding. Watch the trial prompts carefully so you do not accidentally start a paid trial.

Dropbox Sign (formerly HelloSign) — free tier allows limited signature requests per month. Excellent audit trail; integrates with Dropbox if you already use it.

BlueInk, OpenSign, and LibreSign — smaller but legitimate options; OpenSign is open source and can be self-hosted, which means the entire signing flow can run on machines you control. LibreSign is a Nextcloud app for the same reason.

Choosing between them: for one-off personal signing, Option A wins. Choose a service (Option B) when you need: an independent audit trail, multiple signers, or a completed copy sent automatically to a second party. Free tiers all cover that for light use.

Option C: What to avoid

This short list will save you from every real-world mistake in this category:

  • Mystery “free PDF signer” downloads from download-aggregator sites or search ads. Malware is the point, not signing.
  • Browser extensions asking for “read all your email” permissions to help you sign a PDF. Signing never needs your inbox contents.
  • “Free trial” flows that demand a card upfront “to verify” — that is a subscription-trap pattern, and for signing it is never necessary.
  • Apps that convert your signature into a hosted product — if a tiny unknown app stores your signature image on their servers with no obvious company behind it, your signature now exists somewhere you cannot audit. Prefer keeping the signature image on your own device (Chapter 3 shows how).

Making a reusable signature image (the frugal superpower)

Almost every device tool lets you save a signature. Do it once, use it forever:

  1. Sign your name once on plain white paper with a black pen.
  2. Photograph it in good light (or draw it directly on your phone/computer).
  3. Crop to the signature with clear space around it.
  4. Save it as a PNG with a transparent background if your tool offers it (Apple Preview and many apps can; a plain JPEG also works fine on white-form PDFs).
  5. Store it in one known place on your device (Chapter 6 suggests where).

Now you can drop your signature into any PDF in seconds, forever, with zero accounts created. (Reminder: this handbook stops at “how to place it”; the PDF Everyday Handbook covers the full crop/convert/inspect workflow for images.)

A note about very official-looking free options

Some government and healthcare portals ask you to “type your full legal name to sign.” Do not be alarmed — Chapter 1 explained exactly why this is legally real, and Chapter 5 shows how to save evidence copies so the typed-name trail is on your side too.
