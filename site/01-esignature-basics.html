Chapter 1: Is Your E-Signature Actually Legal?

Short answer: yes, in the United States, an electronic signature is as legally binding as a pen signature for the overwhelming majority of everyday documents — leases, insurance enrollments, school forms, medical consents, loan applications, tax filings, employment paperwork, NDAs, purchase agreements.

This chapter explains why, in plain English, so you can sign with confidence.

The two laws that make it work

You do not need to memorize these. You just need to know they exist, because they are the reason nobody can tell you “that e-signature doesn’t count.”

Federal: The ESIGN Act (2000)

The Electronic Signatures in Global and National Commerce Act is a US federal law that says a signature cannot be thrown out just because it is electronic. If you record your agreement electronically (typing your name, drawing it, tapping a confirmation button), ESIGN says that record satisfies the “writing” and “signature” requirements of any law — so long as both parties intended to sign.

Practical takeaway: when you type your name into a form and click “I agree,” that is a signature, not a courtesy tap.

State: UETA (Uniform Electronic Transactions Act)

UETA is a model law adopted (in some form) by most US states. It fills in the details: an electronic signature counts if it is attributable to you (it is connected to your session, your account, or your certificate) and you acted with intent to sign.

A few states add small wrinkles (Illinois, New York, and Washington have their own versions with similar effect). For your everyday documents, the practical rule is identical everywhere: intent + attribution = valid signature.

The three things that make ANY signature valid

Whether you sign with a fountain pen or your finger on a phone screen, courts look for the same three ingredients:

Ingredient What it means What satisfies it
Intent to sign You meant for that mark to be your signature Typing your name, drawing it, tapping a button labeled “Agree” or “Sign”
Consent to transact electronically Both sides agreed this deal happens digitally The request itself (“please sign this e-form”); most tools log your consent explicitly
Attribution The signature is clearly tied to you Your typed name + email + IP + timestamp logged by the tool; or a certificate attached to the signed file

Notice what is not on that list: a specific font, a cursive squiggle, a notarized page, or a particular brand of software. Courts and insurers care about the ingredients above, not the aesthetics.

When e-signatures are NOT enough

Some documents carry special legal requirements that ESIGN and UETA allow to stay on paper or in person. The common ones:

  • Wills, codicils, and most testamentary trusts — most states still require witnesses and sometimes in-person formalities (a few states accept remote e-wills; never assume yours does)
  • Some family-court documents — adoption, certain custody filings vary by state and court
  • Notarized documents — the notarization itself must follow your state’s notary rules (many states now allow remote online notarization, which is electronic and legitimate — the notary verifies your identity on a video call)
  • Certain court filings — courts increasingly accept e-filing, but local rules govern; your court’s clerk page is the source of truth
  • Some real-estate deeds — state-specific requirements on recording and notarization
  • Eviction notices and some statutory notices — occasionally require specific delivery methods

Rule of thumb: for anything involving a will, a court, or a recorder’s office, one web search of “(your state) e-signature requirement + document type” is worth ten minutes before you assume. Everything else — leases, insurance, healthcare consents, school forms, tax filings, employment docs — signs electronically every day without trouble.

“Typed” vs “drawn” vs “digital certificate” signatures

You will meet three legit levels of e-signature. All are legal; they differ in how hard they are to dispute later.

Level 1: Typed or tap-to-agree

You type your name or click “I agree.” Very common on web forms. Legal, and fine for most agreements. The evidence trail lives on the other party’s server (your email, the timestamp, the page you clicked), which is why Chapter 6 tells you to save your own copies.

Level 2: Drawn or uploaded signature on a PDF

You draw your signature with a finger/mouse or sign a paper once, then reuse its image. This is what you do in Adobe Acrobat Reader, Apple Preview, iPhone Markup, or a free tool like SignWell. You get a copy in your own hands — a big upgrade.

Level 3: Certificate-based (cryptographic) signing

Adobe Acrobat Reader can attach a self-signed digital certificate to a PDF. The PDF is then mathematically locked: any later edit makes the signature show “document has been modified.” You can create this certificate yourself, free, in about two minutes (Chapter 3 walks through it). You do not need to buy a certificate from anyone.

Each level up buys you tamper-evidence, not legality. Level 1 is legal; Level 3 is legal and much harder to argue about.

Common myths, quickly retired

  • “It only counts if it’s cursive.” No. Typed names count. So does an “X.” Intent is what counts.
  • “I need expensive software.” No. See Chapter 2: free options cover essentially everything.
  • “Wet ink is more valid than e-signature.” Courts treat them the same under ESIGN/UETA. The evidence quality can differ (Chapter 6 fixes that on your side).
  • “A scanned photo of my signed paper form isn’t a real signature.” It is — it’s an electronic record of your signing, and it validates like Level 2 above.
  • “Neither party can prove what was signed.” True risk — and exactly what chapters 3, 4, and 6 are designed to prevent on your side.

The one-minute pre-sign check

Before any e-signature, run this quick loop. It takes under a minute and covers 95% of the risk you will ever encounter:

  1. Who sent this? If it arrived by email, confirm you know the sender and the request makes sense (Chapter 7 has the full scam-spotting version).
  2. What page count and content? Skim for: money amounts, dates, anything saying “subscription,” “recurring,” “auto-renew,” or “term.”
  3. Am I in the right tool? The request should name a real service you recognize (“via DocuSign,” “via SignWell”) or just come as a plain PDF you can open and sign yourself.
  4. What happens after I sign? You get a completed copy, emailed and/or downloadable. If a service refuses to give you a copy, that is a red flag.

Do that, and you are behaving like a careful, competent signer. The next chapters turn each of these habits into muscle memory.
